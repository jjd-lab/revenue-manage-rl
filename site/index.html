A revenue-management experiment

Both levers, or just the price

A venue sells ten thousand seats for one night over a hundred days. Each day it sets a price and a limit on how many bookings to hold. Should one learned policy set both, or only the price? And does learning beat a textbook planner that works from a forecast? On synthetic demand, the planner wins. Among the learned policies, setting both levers pays off only when training starts from a copy of a simple rule.

In brief

  1. A planner with a forecast beats every learned policy. Planning the whole booking window from the demand model scores 2.22 million, 3 to 8 percent above every learned policy. Of the three wrong demand forecasts tried, the worst cost it 2.5 percent. Its weak spot is expecting more cancellations than really happen: it overbooks every busy night, and a learned policy wins by $116,000 (section 05).
  2. Why learning trails here. The planner is handed the simulator's own model. On the months the learned policies trained on they come within 1.3 to 2.7 percent of it; on the June and December test nights, which they never saw in training, 4.1 to 6.0 percent. Training on all twelve months closes about 40 percent of that gap. Even when a whole year runs 20 percent above or below the forecast, the planner still wins: it re-plans from its own bookings every day (section 05).
  3. Both levers help only with a warm start. Joint BC to SAC, which first copies a simple pricing rule, turns no one away with the cap and still beats the best price-only policy by 3.6 percent, and that gap is real. Plain joint SAC and PPO tie the price-only policies (section 04).
  4. Rules applied after training beat retraining. Blocking price drops at decision time adds $45,000 and removes every markdown; training under the rule loses $110,000. The costs written into the reward decide how hard a policy overbooks, so they are a business choice (section 06).

01 ProblemSell every seat, and no more

Ten thousand seats expire on one night, sold over a hundred-day booking horizon. Demand is stochastic and price-sensitive, and cancellations mean the house can be sold past the seats that exist.

Each day has two controls. Price runs from $80 to $120. The selling limit runs from 10,000 to 15,000. That is the most bookings on hand the venue will accept, so every booking above 10,000 is an overbooking buffer.

Selling past ten thousand is deliberate. People cancel, and some who do not cancel fail to arrive, so a house that stops selling at capacity opens with empty seats. A seat empty on the night is revenue nobody can recover the next morning. Airlines overbook for the same reason. The selling limit is the ceiling on how far past capacity the venue will go, and it is the second thing the agent has to decide. Revenue management calls it a booking limit or an authorization level. The standard treatment is Talluri and van Ryzin (2004).

The question: should one policy set both controls, or only the price, with the limit from a formula? And how does either compare with a planner that works from a forecast?

02 SettingA simulated venue, thirty test nights

Each night is a simulation: demand arrives at the day's price, bookings fill up to the selling limit, some cancel, and some who keep their booking do not come. No box office is in this repository. Policies are compared on thirty held-out nights, June and December, seeds 0 to 29. Thirteen are soft and seventeen are peak. The number under a bar on the charts is that night's seed.

03 PoliciesThree rules, five learners, one cap

Not learned

  • Myopic. Each day, the price that maximizes that day's expected revenue under the true demand model. It optimizes one day at a time and never looks ahead.
  • Floor price. Always $80, limit wide open.
  • Textbook planner. Plans the whole booking window from the demand forecast and the usual cancellation and no-show rates. Each day it picks a price and how many bookings to accept, and it re-plans from the bookings it has actually taken. This is the dynamic-programming approach of Gallego and van Ryzin (1994), and what a revenue-management team would build.

Learned, by trial and error in the simulator

  • PPO. Learns on-policy, from fresh rollouts. Joint PPO sets both controls. Price-only PPO sets the price; an analytic controller sets the limit. Pace PPO is price-only PPO trained with a booking-curve pace penalty and extra weight on soft nights.
  • Joint SAC. An actor-critic that learns off-policy, from a replay buffer, with a bonus for trying actions. It fits two continuous levers.
  • Joint BC to SAC. Starts from a copy of the myopic rule instead of noise (behaviour cloning), then lets SAC improve on the copy. The copy takes both of the rule's numbers, the limit as well as the price. It fills hardest and has the most denied admission.
    1. Copy the myopic rulethree hundred nights of its prices and limits
    2. Let SAC improvethe extra fill, and the denied admission, come from here

Not a policy

  • The cap. A rule applied after training, with no retraining. Once fewer than 20 percent of the seats remain, it moves a joint policy's selling limit most of the way down to the level where expected show-ups would reach 1.02 times capacity. It works out expected show-ups from the usual cancellation and no-show rates, so it is only as good as those rates.

05 PlannerA planner with a forecast wins by 3 to 8 percent

Planning the whole booking window beats every learned policy under the right forecast, under the wrong demand forecasts tried, and under most wrong show-up rates. Its one weak spot: expecting more cancellations than really happen. Neither side carries the cap in this section.

Myopic plans one day at a time. A revenue-management team would plan the whole booking window from a forecast and re-plan as bookings come in: the textbook planner from section 03.

When the cancellation and no-show rates are wrong

The planner leans on its cancellation and no-show rates, and it cannot tell they are wrong until the night itself. Give every policy the same wrong rates:

  • The planner's weak spot. When it expects more cancellations or no-shows than really happen, it overbooks every busy night: it loses 5.8 percent with too many no-shows assumed and 11.8 percent with too many cancellations. The learned policies lose about 3 percent at worst, and win the cancellation case.
  • The wrong demand forecasts tried matter less. A wrong price sensitivity costs myopic up to 5.8 percent and the planner up to 1.35. The learned policies read no forecast and do not move.

Why the learned policies trail

Five retrains that see only what a venue sees, trained on the score’s own costs, narrow down the reasons. One training seed each.

  • Months never seen in training: the largest cause. The learned policies trained on ten months and were tested on June and December. Retrained on all twelve, the same setup trails the planner by 2.9 percent instead of 4.8, closing about 40 percent of the gap.
  • The reward: a small cause. Training on the score's own costs instead of the original reward gained $25,000, a tie.
  • Little room above the planner. It optimizes the score on the simulator's own model; seeing each night's actual no-shows would add only 1.1 percent.
  • A year that misses the forecast does not change it. The planner re-plans from its own bookings every day, so a policy trained to read the year from booking pace still trailed it by 1.6 to 3.8 percent in years 20 percent below or above the forecast.
  • Learning on top of the planner does not help either. Letting a learned policy correct the planner's price and limit each day made it 0.7 to 1.5 percent worse, even when demand arrived 10 days earlier or later than forecast.
  • More data: coverage, not volume. The learned policies already see about two thousand simulated nights per run. What helps is experience of every season: for a real venue, at least a full year of history.

Details: sections 14 to 19 of the experiment log.

06 VariationsBlock markdowns; choose the reward's costs

Two changes to the setup, each tried on Joint SAC: a rule that the price never falls, and a different price list in the training reward.

The late markdown was losing money

In the price-path chart, the weekend price falls from $117 to $89 in the last weeks, so a late buyer pays less than an early one. A venue cannot do that to its early buyers. Four ways to stop it, against no rule:

The price may rise, but never fall

How Score Markdowns
No rule 2.033 1,672
Block price drops at decision time, no retraining 2.078 0
Copy the blocked policy, no further training 2.039 0
A fine for each drop (best of seven strengths) 2.049 1,377
Train a new policy under the rule 1.923 0

Scores are in millions, on the thirty nights. Markdowns are days the charged price fell. The block overrides the policy on 2,610 of its 3,000 decisions and still scores higher, with the paired interval above zero.

Block, don't retrain. The late markdown was losing money. Training under the rule makes the policy open cheap and stop moving the price, and a fine does not stop the drops. Details: runs/price_monotone_up/.

The reward decides the overbooking

The training reward in section 02 is a price list: what each mistake costs the policy. We changed two prices and trained Joint SAC again, same settings, same seed.

Same policy, two price lists

Original reward New reward
An empty seat costs $650, and it misses the $80 bonus $200
A denied admission costs $450, and the first one loses the whole bonus $400
Peak nights that turn people away 3 of 17 11 of 17
Score 2.033 2.088
Score with the cap 2.009 2.096

Scores are in millions. The paired interval on the gain sits above zero.

  • Why it changes. Under the original reward the first person turned away costs the whole bonus, so the policy keeps a safety margin. At a flat $400, it overbooks whenever it expects enough no-shows. The gain is all on busy nights.
  • The costs are a business choice. What an empty seat and a turned-away ticket holder cost is a judgement about the venue: lost concessions, refunds, compensation, reputation. Put those costs in the reward, and the policy follows them. Change them, and you get a different policy.
  • The catch. The cap no longer gets denied admission to zero, and the score charges the same $400 per denied admission as the new reward. If turning a ticket holder away really costs the venue more than about $650, the old reward wins. One training run. Details: runs/objective/.

07 Which to usePick by what you know

When Use
You can build a demand forecast and estimate cancellation and no-show rates The textbook planner. It leads every learned policy under the right forecast, the wrong demand forecasts tried, and most wrong show-up rates. If you may be overestimating cancellations, add the cap: it lowers the selling limit near the end and removes that weak spot.
Before training any policy Set the costs in its reward to what an empty seat and a turned-away ticket holder really cost your venue. The policy will follow them.
A learned policy that turns almost no one away Joint BC to SAC with the cap. Denied admission falls to zero on the published weights, but not on every retrain, and only while the cap's rates are right.
The policy should set only the price Pace PPO. An analytic controller sets the selling limit, and denied admission on these thirty nights is zero.
Later buyers must never pay less than earlier ones Keep the policy and block price drops at decision time. It scores higher than the same policy unblocked, with no retraining.
No demand forecast can be built at all A learned joint policy. It reads bookings and the calendar, not a demand model.
You cannot estimate cancellations and no-shows at all Nothing here is safe. Every policy overbooks when those rates are overestimated, and the cap does too.

08 LimitsWhat this does not show

  • The sample. The demand model is synthetic. There is one product and one venue. Every chart uses the same thirty held-out nights, seeds 0 to 29, in June and December. Train the same policy again on another machine and the weights will not match bit for bit.
  • Ties. A gap ranks two policies only when its paired interval excludes zero. Capped Joint BC to SAC over pace does, at 3.6 percent. Joint SAC and Joint PPO against pace do not.
  • Training seeds. The published Joint BC to SAC and pace PPO weights are training seed 42; Joint SAC is seed 7 and Joint PPO seed 49. Retrains of Joint BC to SAC at seeds 43 and 44 also beat a pace run from the same seed. The retrain at seed 46 ties its matched pace run. Most results on this page are one training run.
  • The show-up count. The published learned policies and the cap read a count of expected show-ups built from the true cancellation and no-show rates, which no venue has. With the venue's own estimate and the right rates, scores move by under 0.1 percent. With wrong rates, every policy overbooks more, and the cap no longer holds denied admission at zero. The retrains in section 05 see only the venue's estimate.
  • Test months never seen in training. The learned policies train on ten months and are scored only on June and December, which they never saw; the planner's forecast covers every month. That split tests generalization. Retrained on all twelve months, the gap to the planner falls from 4.8 to 2.9 percent (section 05, one training seed). A venue would train on every month it has history for.
  • The planner's advantage. The planner is handed the simulator's own demand model, and day-to-day demand here is fairly predictable. Its forecast was made wrong in these ways: price sensitivity off by a quarter either way, weekend and peak demand 25 percent too low, and random errors of up to 25 percent on each night, a whole year 20 percent above or below forecast, and demand arriving 10 days early or late. A planner that re-plans from its own bookings absorbed all of these. Not tested: customers who wait for a lower price, demand that depends on past prices, and problems too large to plan exactly, such as several seat tiers sharing one house. Those are where a learned policy could still come out ahead.
  • Myopic. Its selling limit, 12,353, never binds: it holds at most 11,402 bookings. That is why pace PPO, not myopic, is the price-only comparison.

09 BackgroundWhere this sits

Quantity control protects seats at given fares, from Littlewood (1972) through Belobaba's expected marginal seat revenue heuristics. Dynamic pricing sets the price path from capacity left, as in Gallego and van Ryzin (1994). McGill and van Ryzin (1999) survey both lines. Gosavi, Bandla, and Das (2002) reported gains over a nested-EMSR heuristic with reinforcement learning, in a simulation with discrete fare classes. This experiment is not that benchmark.

Four differences from that line:

  1. Both controls, one policy. Those two literatures usually keep the levers apart. One sets booking limits at posted fares. The other sets a price path given the seats left. Here one agent sets price and the selling limit together. A price-only twin, run on the same nights, measures what dropping the limit costs.
  2. No demand forecast in view. Littlewood, EMSR, and Gallego and van Ryzin all need one. The learned agents see only where bookings stand and what day it is. They still learn two price paths. On weekends the price rises toward the date, then falls. On weekdays it eases down to $80. The planner in section 05 is the forecast-based comparison.
  3. Empty seats are not the main measure. Counting empty seats would call every soft night a failure. But on this demand, even the $80 floor with no selling limit leaves each soft night more than 1,500 seats empty, so no policy can fill them. The score charges for empty seats only on the peak nights.
  4. Overbooking is priced in training and capped only after it. Classical overbooking is designed into the optimization. Here the training reward charges for denied admission but does not forbid it, so a trained policy may overbook. A cap applied afterwards, with no retraining, takes denied admission to zero on the published weights, as long as the cancellation and no-show rates it uses are right.
