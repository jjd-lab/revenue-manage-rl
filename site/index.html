A revenue-management experiment

Both levers, or just the price

One policy sets the ticket price and the selling limit together. Another sets only the price. On a fictional amphitheater, with synthetic demand, the joint policies win on revenue. They do not win by filling the quiet nights.

01Problem

Ten thousand seats expire on one night, sold over a hundred-day booking horizon. Demand is stochastic and price-sensitive, and cancellations mean the house can be sold past the seats that exist.

Each day has two controls. Price runs from $80 to $120. The selling limit runs from 10,000 to 15,000. That is the most bookings on hand the venue will accept, so every booking above 10,000 is an overbooking buffer.

Selling past ten thousand is deliberate. People cancel, and some who do not cancel fail to arrive, so a house that stops selling at capacity opens with empty seats. A seat empty on the night is revenue nobody can recover the next morning. Airlines overbook for the same reason. The selling limit is the ceiling on how far past capacity the venue will go, and it is the second thing the agent has to decide. Revenue management calls it a booking limit or an authorization level. The standard treatment is Talluri and van Ryzin (2004).

Maximize revenue, charged for both ways of getting the night wrong: denied admission, more show-ups than seats, and spoilage, seats empty when the curtain rises. One policy for both controls, or price only, with the limit from a formula?

02Where this sits

Quantity control protects seats at given fares, from Littlewood (1972) through Belobaba's expected marginal seat revenue heuristics. Dynamic pricing sets the price path from capacity left, as in Gallego and van Ryzin (1994). McGill and van Ryzin (1999) survey both lines. Gosavi, Bandla, and Das (2002) reported gains over a nested-EMSR heuristic with reinforcement learning, in a simulation with discrete fare classes. This experiment is not that benchmark.

Four differences from that line:

  1. Both controls, one policy. Those two literatures usually keep the levers apart. One sets booking limits at posted fares. The other sets a price path given the seats left. Here one agent sets price and the selling limit together. A price-only twin, run on the same nights, measures what dropping the limit costs.
  2. No demand forecast in view. Littlewood, EMSR, and Gallego and van Ryzin all need one. The agent sees only where bookings stand and what day it is. It still learns two price paths. On weekends the price rises toward the date, then falls. On weekdays it eases down to $80.
  3. Spoilage is the wrong headline. Occupancy would treat empty seats as the failure. On this demand, even $80 with the house wide open still leaves every quiet night more than 1,500 seats short. Remaining inventory is not how the policies are ranked.
  4. Overbooking is priced in training and blocked only after it. Classical overbooking is designed into the optimization. Here the training reward charges for denied admission but does not forbid it, so a trained policy may overbook. A cap applied afterwards, with no retraining, is what takes denied admission to zero.

03The policies

Not learned

  • Myopic. Each day, the price that maximizes this period's expected revenue under the true demand model. That is what a forecast-and-optimize rule does with a perfect forecast.
  • Floor price. Always $80, limit wide open.

Learned

  • PPO. Learns on-policy, from fresh rollouts. Joint PPO sets both controls. Price-only PPO sets the price; an analytic controller sets the limit. Pace PPO is price-only PPO trained with a booking-curve pace penalty and extra weight on soft nights.
  • Joint SAC. An actor-critic that learns off-policy, from a replay buffer, with a bonus for trying actions. It fits two continuous levers.
  • Joint BC to SAC. Starts from the myopic rule instead of noise, then lets SAC improve on the copy. The imitation takes both of the expert's numbers, the limit as well as the price. It fills hardest and has the most denied admission.
    1. Copy the myopic rulethree hundred nights of the forecast-and-optimize rule
    2. Let SAC improvethe extra fill, and the denied admission, come from here

Not a policy

  • The cap. A projection applied after training. When a joint policy's limit would let expected show-ups pass 1.02 times capacity, the limit is lowered. No retraining.

05Findings

The bars below are thirteen of the thirty held-out nights. The number under each bar is that night's seed: 1, 2, 3, 5, 9, 11, 14, 16, 17, 19, 21, 24, and 26. The other seventeen seeds are the peak nights, and this chart leaves them out. The height of each bar is unsold seats.

The late markdown was losing money

Look again at the weekend line above. It rises to $117 around day 37, then falls to $89. A buyer on the last day pays less than a buyer three weeks earlier. A real venue cannot do that to the people who booked early.

So we forbade it. The rule is: the price may never go down. It may still go up.

Blocking the drops does not cost anything. It earns more. Take the trained policy, leave it exactly as it is, and refuse any price below yesterday's price. The score goes from 2,033,000 to 2,078,000, and the paired interval on that gain sits above zero. The weekend path now climbs from $108.50 to $118.20 and stays there. No retraining, and no markdown on any of the thirty nights.

The block is doing a lot of work. It overrides the policy on 2,610 of its 3,000 daily decisions. The policy keeps asking to cut the price, and it is wrong to ask every time.

Training a policy under the rule is a different story, and a worse one. Every attempt scored about 110,000 below the blocked policy. A retrained policy stops moving the price at all: $116.31 on every weekend day from a hundred days out to the last, and the $80 floor on weekdays. Under a rule that only lets the price rise, opening cheap is the move that keeps the most options open, so that is what it learns. Copying the blocked policy gets most of the way back, to 2,039,000 with no markdowns, but only if you stop there. Training on top of the copy undid it every time we tried.

The training did not fail. It did what it was told. The copy earns 54.6 on the training reward, and after training it earns 96.2. Over the same steps the score fell from 2,039,000 to 1,967,000. The reward and the score disagree about seats. The reward charges about $730 for any unsold seat. On a night with even one denied admission, it also takes away the whole bonus for filling the house. The score charges nothing for an unsold seat on a soft night. On a peak night it charges $200 for an unsold seat and $400 for a denied admission. So training dropped the soft nights to the $80 floor to fill 831 more seats, which cost $10,900 a night. On peak nights the copy overbooked 8 of 17. Training stopped that by selling fewer tickets, and gave up $127,000 of peak revenue a night. Holding the training closer to the copy would only slow that slide, so we did not try it.

A fine instead of a block does not work. We tried four strengths. The best one still cut the price 1,377 times. If the promise is that later buyers never pay less, it has to be a block. Details: runs/price_monotone_up/.

The reward decides the overbooking

Every policy on this page learned from the same reward. It charges about $730 for an unsold seat, which is more than the ticket. On a night with even one denied admission, it also takes away the whole bonus for filling the house. The score is gentler: $200 for an unsold seat on a peak night, and $400 for each denied admission.

So we trained Joint SAC again with one change: a simpler reward. Revenue, minus $200 for each unsold seat and $400 for each denied admission, on every night. A venue could write that rule down. It does not need to know in advance which nights are quiet. Same settings, same training seed.

The score goes from 2,033,000 to 2,088,000, and the paired interval on that gain sits above zero. All of the gain is on peak nights, and it comes from overbooking. The new policy sells past capacity on 11 of 17 peak nights, up from 3. It charges more and leaves fewer seats empty. Quiet nights do not change. Each policy scores best on the reward it trained on.

With the cap, the new policy scores 2,096,000, close to capped Joint BC to SAC. But the cap no longer takes denied admission to zero. Two peak nights still turn people away. That is why it is not in the table above.

Read the reward as a price list. Each charge is what the policy is told a mistake costs, and the policy acts on those prices. Changing the price of an empty seat did nothing on quiet nights, because those nights cannot fill at any price. Changing the price of a denied admission changed how often the policy turned people away.

The score is a price list too. It charges $400 for a denied admission, the same as the new reward. The new policy turns away about 216 more people per peak night than Joint SAC, so every extra dollar per denied admission costs it about 216 points. Its lead is gone at about $652. If turning a ticket holder away costs the venue less than that, counting refunds, compensation and the lost customer, the new reward is the better choice. If it costs more, the old one is. This is one training run. Details: runs/objective/.

What if the forecast is wrong? The myopic rule and the selling-limit controllers use the same demand model that generates the nights. A real venue does not get that model. If that forecast is wrong about price sensitivity by a plausible amount, myopic alone loses 3.5 to 5.8 percent. Capped Joint BC to SAC over pace is 3.6 percent, and that interval sits above zero. The joint policies do not move. Scores stay the same, and mean prices stay the same to the cent, because those policies never read a demand model. They see bookings and the calendar.

A joint policy does not need the forecast to be right, because it does not use a forecast. A forecast that is wrong only about the level of demand changes no price, for any policy here. Demand enters as a multiple, so that level cancels out of the sum that picks a price. Only a wrong price sensitivity changes the price.

06Limits

  • The sample. The demand model is synthetic. There is one product and one venue. Every chart uses the same thirty held-out nights, seeds 0 to 29, in June and December. Train the same policy again on another machine and the weights will not match bit for bit.
  • Capped Joint BC to SAC. On the published training it scores 3.6 percent higher than pace. Resample those thirty nights and the paired interval on that gap sits above zero. The paired interval is the range of the gap across resamples. The gap ranks the two policies only when that range excludes zero.
  • Joint SAC and Joint PPO. The paired interval against pace covers zero for both. These thirty nights do not say which policy in either pair scores higher.
  • Training seeds. The published weights are training seed 42. Retrains at seeds 43 and 44 also beat a pace run from the same seed. The retrain at seed 46 ties its matched pace run. It scores below the published pace checkpoint. On seeds 43 and 44 the cap leaves denied admission on 0.24 and 0.47 of peak nights.
  • Earlier nights. On an earlier set of thirty nights, Joint SAC had the highest score among the joint policies. This page uses seeds 0 to 29. On these nights Joint SAC is not first.
  • Myopic. It sets the selling limit at 12,353. The most bookings it holds is 11,402. Raise that limit to 15,000 and revenue stays the same to the cent. At a price of $80, bookings reach 11,402. At myopic's price of $92, they stay below the limit. Pace PPO is the price-only comparison on this page because it sets only the price.
  • Soft nights. On the thirteen soft nights, the best price with the selling limit wide open still leaves more than 1,500 seats empty. Those seats stay empty at every price on the grid.
  • The cap. It lowers the selling limit after training. It does not retrain the policy. The reward already charges for denied admission during training. A policy trained with the cap in place is a different run. This page does not report one.
