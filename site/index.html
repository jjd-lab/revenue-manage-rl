A revenue-management experiment

Both levers, or just the price

One policy sets the ticket price and the selling limit together. Another sets only the price. On a fictional amphitheater, with synthetic demand, the joint policies earn the most of the learned policies, and all of the extra comes from the busy nights. A textbook planner, which plans the whole booking window from a forecast, beats every one of them, even with a wrong demand forecast. The learned policies come out ahead when the planner overestimates cancellations or no-shows.

01Problem

Ten thousand seats expire on one night, sold over a hundred-day booking horizon. Demand is stochastic and price-sensitive, and cancellations mean the house can be sold past the seats that exist.

Each day has two controls. Price runs from $80 to $120. The selling limit runs from 10,000 to 15,000. That is the most bookings on hand the venue will accept, so every booking above 10,000 is an overbooking buffer.

Selling past ten thousand is deliberate. People cancel, and some who do not cancel fail to arrive, so a house that stops selling at capacity opens with empty seats. A seat empty on the night is revenue nobody can recover the next morning. Airlines overbook for the same reason. The selling limit is the ceiling on how far past capacity the venue will go, and it is the second thing the agent has to decide. Revenue management calls it a booking limit or an authorization level. The standard treatment is Talluri and van Ryzin (2004).

Maximize revenue, charged for both ways of getting the night wrong: denied admission, more show-ups than seats, and spoilage, seats empty when the curtain rises. One policy for both controls, or price only, with the limit from a formula?

02Where this sits

Quantity control protects seats at given fares, from Littlewood (1972) through Belobaba's expected marginal seat revenue heuristics. Dynamic pricing sets the price path from capacity left, as in Gallego and van Ryzin (1994). McGill and van Ryzin (1999) survey both lines. Gosavi, Bandla, and Das (2002) reported gains over a nested-EMSR heuristic with reinforcement learning, in a simulation with discrete fare classes. This experiment is not that benchmark.

Four differences from that line:

  1. Both controls, one policy. Those two literatures usually keep the levers apart. One sets booking limits at posted fares. The other sets a price path given the seats left. Here one agent sets price and the selling limit together. A price-only twin, run on the same nights, measures what dropping the limit costs.
  2. No demand forecast in view. Littlewood, EMSR, and Gallego and van Ryzin all need one. The agent sees only where bookings stand and what day it is. It still learns two price paths. On weekends the price rises toward the date, then falls. On weekdays it eases down to $80.
  3. Empty seats are not the main measure. Counting empty seats would call every quiet night a failure. But on this demand, even the $80 floor with no selling limit leaves each quiet night more than 1,500 seats empty, so no policy can fill them. The score charges for empty seats only on the busy nights.
  4. Overbooking is priced in training and blocked only after it. Classical overbooking is designed into the optimization. Here the training reward charges for denied admission but does not forbid it, so a trained policy may overbook. A cap applied afterwards, with no retraining, takes denied admission to zero, as long as the cancellation and no-show rates it uses are right.

03The policies

Not learned

  • Myopic. Each day, the price that maximizes this period's expected revenue under the true demand model. That is what a forecast-and-optimize rule does with a perfect forecast.
  • Floor price. Always $80, limit wide open.
  • Textbook planner. Plans the whole booking window from the demand forecast and the usual cancellation and no-show rates. Each day it picks a price and how many bookings to accept, and it re-plans from the bookings it has actually taken. This is the dynamic-programming approach of Gallego and van Ryzin (1994), and what a revenue-management team would build. Section 06 compares it with the learned policies.

Learned

  • PPO. Learns on-policy, from fresh rollouts. Joint PPO sets both controls. Price-only PPO sets the price; an analytic controller sets the limit. Pace PPO is price-only PPO trained with a booking-curve pace penalty and extra weight on soft nights.
  • Joint SAC. An actor-critic that learns off-policy, from a replay buffer, with a bonus for trying actions. It fits two continuous levers.
  • Joint BC to SAC. Starts from the myopic rule instead of noise, then lets SAC improve on the copy. The imitation takes both of the expert's numbers, the limit as well as the price. It fills hardest and has the most denied admission.
    1. Copy the myopic rulethree hundred nights of the forecast-and-optimize rule
    2. Let SAC improvethe extra fill, and the denied admission, come from here

Not a policy

  • The cap. A projection applied after training. When a joint policy's limit would let expected show-ups pass 1.02 times capacity, the limit is lowered. No retraining. It works out expected show-ups from the usual cancellation and no-show rates, so it is only as good as those rates.

04Setting

Each night is a simulation. No box office is in this repository. Thirty held-out nights, June and December, seeds 0 to 29. Thirteen are soft. Seventeen are peak. The number under a bar on the charts is that night's seed.

Every learned policy in the main comparison trains on this reward. Pace PPO adds a pace penalty and extra weight on soft nights. This reward is not the score. It charges for empty seats on every night, and at more than three times the score's rate. A single denied admission also costs the whole bonus. The score below is what the policies are compared on.

An oversold seat is a denied admission, a show-up with no seat. Every policy on this page beats a price of $80 on the soft nights, so the soft part equals soft-night revenue. A difference in the score is a ranking only when the paired interval excludes zero.

06Against a textbook planner

Every learned policy above scores at least as well as myopic. But myopic plans one day at a time. A revenue-management team would plan the whole booking window from a forecast, and re-plan as bookings come in. That is the textbook planner from section 03. It is not learned, and it needs no training.

The planner against the best learned policy

What the planner knows Planner Best learned Planner ahead
The right forecast 2.219 2.081 +0.138
A wrong demand forecast (worst of three) 2.164 2.081 +0.083
Only the usual numbers, and every night is different 2.121 2.022 +0.099

Scores are in millions. Best learned is Joint BC to SAC with the cap in the first two rows. It does not use a forecast, so a wrong one does not move it. In the last row, each night draws its own demand level, price sensitivity, no-show rate and cancellation rate, and Joint SAC was retrained on nights like that. The planner is told only the usual values. The paired interval sits above zero in the first and last rows. The middle row has no interval.

Where the lead comes from: on busy nights the planner aims at a full house and misses by about 90 seats. The learned policies leave 150 to 420 seats empty, or turn hundreds of people away. On quiet nights the planner charges $92, the price that earns the most when a night cannot fill. The learned policies charge $82 to $84, because their training reward charges about $730 for every empty seat.

Where the learned policies hold up better

The planner leans on its cancellation and no-show rates. It plans the whole night around how many bookings will turn into show-ups, and it cannot tell it is wrong until the night itself. When those rates are off the same way on every night, the gap closes, and in one direction it flips. Every policy below is shown the same wrong estimate.

When the cancellation and no-show rates are wrong

The rates the policies are given Planner Best learned Ahead
Fewer no-shows than real 2.127 2.088 Planner
Fewer cancellations than real 2.108 2.094 Planner, narrowly
More no-shows than real 2.091 2.105 Learned, narrowly
More cancellations than real 1.958 2.074 Learned, by 116,000

Scores are in millions. No-show rates are moved by 4 points and the cancellation curve by a similar amount, the kind of error a venue estimating from its own history could make. Best learned is the highest of the three learned policies in each row. No paired intervals were computed for these rows.

When the planner expects more cancellations or no-shows than really happen, it overbooks every busy night and turns hundreds of people away. The learned policies overbook too, but far less: their worst case costs them about 3 percent, against the planner's 12. The likely reason is that they react to the bookings they actually see, rather than follow a plan built on the wrong rates.

Two more things in the learned policies' favour. They got within about 6 percent of a planner that was handed the true demand model, without ever being told it. And part of that 6 percent is their training reward, not the learning: changing the reward alone added $54,000 (section 07).

The learned policies need no demand forecast at all. A plausibly wrong price sensitivity costs myopic 3.5 to 5.8 percent and leaves the learned policies exactly where they were. The planner does use a forecast, but it re-plans from the bookings it sees, so the worst wrong forecast tried cost it 2.5 percent. On this simulator, needing no forecast is worth less than planning. Details: runs/dp_baseline/ and runs/uncertain_nights/.

07Variations

Two changes to the setup, each tried on Joint SAC: a rule that the price may never fall, and a different training reward.

The late markdown was losing money

Look again at the weekend line in the price-path chart above. It rises to $117 around day 37, then falls to $89, so a buyer on the last day pays less than one who booked three weeks earlier. A venue cannot do that to its early buyers. So we added a rule: the price may rise, but never fall.

Blocking the drops earns more. Keep the trained policy as it is and refuse any price below yesterday's. The score goes from 2,033,000 to 2,078,000, and the paired interval on that gain sits above zero. The block overrides 2,610 of the policy's 3,000 daily decisions, and none of the thirty nights has a markdown.

Training a policy under the rule does worse, by about 110,000. The retrained policy stops moving the price and opens cheap, because under a rule that only lets the price rise, opening cheap keeps the most options open. Copying the blocked policy gets back to 2,039,000 with no markdowns, but further training undoes it. That training follows the reward, which charges about $730 for every empty seat. So it drops quiet nights to the $80 floor and sells fewer tickets on busy nights to avoid overbooking, and both cost score.

A fine instead of a block does not work. The best of four strengths still cut the price 1,377 times. Details: runs/price_monotone_up/.

The reward decides the overbooking

The training reward in the Setting section is a price list: it tells the policy what each mistake costs. We changed two of those prices and trained Joint SAC again, with the same settings and the same training seed. The new prices are ones a venue could write down without knowing in advance which nights will be quiet.

Same policy, two price lists

Original reward New reward
An empty seat costs $730 $200
A denied admission costs $450, and the first one loses the whole bonus $400
Peak nights that turn people away 3 of 17 11 of 17
Score 2.033 2.088
Score with the cap 2.009 2.096

Scores are in millions. The empty-seat price in the original reward is the $650 charge plus the $80 of bonus the seat does not earn. The paired interval on the 0.055 gain sits above zero.

Under the original reward, the first person turned away costs the whole bonus, so the policy keeps a safety margin. Under the new one, each denied admission costs a flat $400, so the policy overbooks whenever it expects enough no-shows. That is where the gain comes from: peak nights, which sell more and leave fewer seats empty. Quiet nights barely change, because they cannot fill at any price.

Two warnings. First, the cap no longer takes denied admission to zero: two peak nights still turn people away, which is why the new policy is not in the table above. Second, the score also charges $400 for a denied admission, the same as the new reward. The new policy turns away about 216 more people per peak night, so its lead disappears if a denied admission really costs the venue more than about $652, counting refunds, compensation and the lost customer. This is one training run. Details: runs/objective/.

08Which policy to use

When Use
Highest score, and you know your demand and show-up rates reasonably well The textbook planner. It leads every learned policy, including when its forecast is off.
A learned policy that turns no one away Joint BC to SAC with the cap, as long as the cancellation and no-show rates the cap uses are right.
The policy should set only the price Pace PPO. An analytic controller sets the selling limit, and denied admission on these thirty nights is zero.
Later buyers must never pay less than earlier ones Keep the policy and block price drops at decision time. It scores higher than the same policy unblocked, with no retraining.
You may be overestimating cancellations or no-shows A learned joint policy. When those rates are off in that direction, it holds up far better than the planner and scores higher.
You cannot estimate cancellations and no-shows at all Nothing here is safe. Every policy overbooks when those rates are overestimated, and the cap does too.

09Limits

  • The sample. The demand model is synthetic. There is one product and one venue. Every chart uses the same thirty held-out nights, seeds 0 to 29, in June and December. Train the same policy again on another machine and the weights will not match bit for bit.
  • Ties. A gap ranks two policies only when its paired interval excludes zero. Capped Joint BC to SAC over pace does, at 3.6 percent. Joint SAC and Joint PPO against pace do not.
  • Training seeds. The published weights are training seed 42. Retrains at seeds 43 and 44 also beat a pace run from the same seed. The retrain at seed 46 ties its matched pace run. Most results on this page are one training run.
  • The show-up count. The learned policies and the cap read a count of expected show-ups built from the true cancellation and no-show rates, which no venue has. With the venue's own estimate and the right rates, scores move by under 0.1 percent. With wrong rates, every policy overbooks more, and the cap no longer holds denied admission at zero.
  • The planner's advantage. Day-to-day demand here is fairly predictable, which suits a planner. Night-level errors were random. A whole season running quiet was not tested.
  • Myopic. Its selling limit, 12,353, never binds: it holds at most 11,402 bookings. That is why pace PPO, not myopic, is the price-only comparison.
