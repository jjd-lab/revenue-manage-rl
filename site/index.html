A revenue-management experiment

Both levers, or just the price

One policy sets the ticket price and the selling limit together. Another sets only the price. On a fictional amphitheater, with synthetic demand, the joint policies win — and not by filling the quiet nights.

01Problem

Ten thousand seats expire on one night, sold over a hundred-day booking horizon. Demand is stochastic and price-sensitive, and cancellations mean the house can be sold past the seats that exist.

Two controls each day. Price, $80 to $120. The selling limit, 10,000 to 15,000: the most bookings on hand the venue will accept, so everything above 10,000 is an overbooking buffer.

Selling past ten thousand is deliberate. People cancel, and some who do not cancel fail to arrive, so a house that stops selling at capacity opens with empty seats — and a seat empty on the night is revenue nobody can recover the next morning. Airlines overbook for the same reason. The selling limit is the ceiling on how far past capacity the venue will go, and it is the second thing the agent has to decide. Revenue management calls it a booking limit or an authorization level; the standard treatment is Talluri and van Ryzin (2004).

Maximize revenue, charged for both ways of getting the night wrong: denied admission, more show-ups than seats, and spoilage, seats empty when the curtain rises. One policy for both controls, or price only, with the limit from a formula?

In the code and the tables: selling_limit, remain_inv (negative means denied admission), oversell.

02Where this sits

Quantity control protects seats at given fares — Littlewood (1972), then Belobaba’s expected marginal seat revenue heuristics. Dynamic pricing sets the price path from capacity left — Gallego and van Ryzin (1994). McGill and van Ryzin (1999) survey both lines. Gosavi, Bandla, and Das (2002) reported gains over a nested-EMSR heuristic with reinforcement learning, in a simulation with discrete fare classes. This is not that benchmark.

  1. Both controls, one policy. Price-only is run on the same nights, so the cost of dropping the limit is measured.
  2. No demand forecast in view. The agent sees only where bookings stand and what day it is, and still learns a booking curve.
  3. Spoilage is the wrong headline. At the floor price, every soft date is still more than 1,500 seats short.
  4. Overbooking risk after training. A cap, not a new reward, cuts peak denied admission from 0.71 of nights to none.

03The policies

  • Myopic. Not learned. Each day, the price that maximizes this period’s expected revenue under the true demand model — what a forecast-and-optimize rule does with a perfect forecast.
  • Floor price. Not learned. Always $80, limit wide open.
  • PPO. Learns on-policy, from fresh rollouts. Joint PPO sets both controls. Price-only PPO sets the price; an analytic controller sets the limit. Pace PPO is price-only PPO trained with a booking-curve pace penalty and extra weight on soft nights.
  • Joint SAC. An actor-critic that learns off-policy, from a replay buffer, with a bonus for trying actions. It fits two continuous levers.
  • BC to SAC. Behaviour cloning: watch the myopic rule play three hundred nights and copy it, so training does not start from noise, then let SAC improve on the copy. The imitation takes both of the expert’s numbers, the limit as well as the price. It fills hardest and oversells most.
  • The cap. Not a policy but a projection applied after training: when a joint policy’s limit would let expected show-ups pass 1.02 times capacity, the limit is lowered. No retraining.
  1. Expert rollouts
  2. Clone the actor
  3. Seed the buffer
  4. Fine-tune

04Setting

Demand is synthetic. A tree predicts base demand from the calendar and from how far out the night is; price then bends it linearly around $100. Cancellations are Weibull. No-shows run near 16 percent. No real box office is in this repository.

Thirty held-out nights, June and December, seeds 0 to 29 — the same thirty nights for every policy, every table, and every chart on this page. They split into thirteen soft nights and seventeen peak nights. The score adds a peak part and a soft part. Peak: revenue, less $200 for every seat left unsold and $400 for every seat oversold. Soft: revenue, less its gap to a floor-price oracle — and that gap is zero for every policy published here. So peak revenue, and denied admission, decide the order.

05Findings

One more check, because the page’s title depends on it. Is the second control actually doing the work, or does the joint policy simply price better? Take the limit away — pin it wide open, or pin it to the flat rule the myopic policy uses — and leave the price untouched. Every joint policy loses between $90,000 and $344,000 of score, far more than the 1.2 to 3.6 percent that separates joint from price-only. Denied admission on peak nights goes from 0.18 to 0.82 the moment the limit stops moving.

The flat-rule arm is the one to sit with. Joint SAC’s limit averages 12,578, within two percent of the flat 12,353 it is pinned to — and that pinning still costs $120,000. The value is not where the limit sits. It is when it moves. (Those pinned policies are priced for a limit they no longer have, so this shows how tightly the two controls are coupled; the honest measure of the second lever against a purpose-built one-lever policy remains the 1.2 to 3.6 percent above.)

  • Best score with no denied admission. BC to SAC, plus the cap.
  • Highest raw score, if denied admission is acceptable. BC to SAC.
  • The one whose behaviour is easiest to read. Joint SAC — the booking curve and the calendar premium above are its.
  • One lever, no denied admission. Pace PPO.

06Limits

One synthetic demand model, one product, one venue, one set of thirty seeds. Training is not bit-for-bit the same on another machine. An earlier draw of thirty nights put joint SAC first among the three joint policies; their order among themselves is within seed noise, their lead over every price-only policy held in both draws. The ranking belongs to this environment.

The myopic rule also flatters the comparison, in a way worth stating plainly. It nominally sets both controls, but its limit never binds: the limit sits at 12,353 while the rule never holds more than 11,402 bookings at once — a ceiling it never reaches, like a credit limit well above anything you ever spend. Move that limit anywhere from there up to the 15,000 cap and the revenue is identical to the cent. So myopic is really a price-only policy with a second control that does nothing, and beating it is not by itself an answer to the two-lever question. Pace PPO, price-only by construction, is the honest one-lever benchmark: the joint policies clear it by 1.2 to 3.6 percent. Nothing is wrong with the myopic rule — at $80 rather than its own $92 the same venue presses the same ceiling hard — its price simply keeps it away from the constraint.

What travels is the method: check an oracle ceiling before trusting a fill rate, check whether a baseline’s constraints bind before crediting them, and keep overbooking risk in a projection rather than in the reward.
